Drive Reduction Theory

Drive: A state of arousal or discomfort triggered by an unmet physiological or biological need. Common examples include hunger, thirst, and the need for warmth. When a drive emerges, an agent feels an unpleasant tension. They behave in ways to reduce that tension. To reduce the tension they feel, they will seek out ways to satisfy their biological needs. Every unmet need pushes an agent to act, reduce the drive, and restore that balance.

Primary Drives: Innate biological needs such as being hungry or thirsty. Directly applicable to biological survival.
Secondary Drives: Learned through conditioning or association with a primary drive, such as social acceptance, teamwork, barter/trade. Contributes to species survival instead of individual survival. 
To minimize the discomfort that is being caused by primary drives such as hunger, one may go hunting, butcher the kill, cook it, and then eat it. Reward size and desirability, independent of any underlying deprivation, shape how vigorously a behavior is performed. 

Fear: Arises from conditions or potential conditions which may threaten drive reduction but that an agent feels/believes they cannot successfully fight or resist. Reducing fear reinforces behavior in the same way reducing hunger does. 
Anger: Arises from conditions or potential conditions which may threaten drive reduction but that an agent feels/believes they can successfully fight or resist. 
Overindulgence: Agents engage in actions well past the point their need is satisfied. Used to attempt to get the “reward” of satisfying a drive when there is another drive which cannot be easily satisfied. 
Thrill-Seeking: Agents engage in behavior that increases discomfort to indirectly increase the odds of future drive satisfaction (primary and secondary). Can mimic the reward received when a dangerous situation is avoided or defeated. 
Arousal and Performance: Very high arousal can make performance worse rather than better. Arises from an agent that is unsure if they will be able to satisfy drive demand, but the possible patterns do not pose any immediate danger to an agent. High arousal can cause an agent who is highly confident in their success to focus on the best pattern and successful alternatives should a primary pattern look likely to fail. 
Growth: Actions which strengthen future drive satisfaction (primary and secondary) potential.

Pattern Recognition (Conditioning): A response like fishing becomes more likely not because a reward feels pleasant, but because it reduces a drive or potentially reduces a drive. Subsequent successful drive reduction events using the same method reinforces the pattern. The drive reduction functions as a reinforcement for whatever behavioral pattern satisfied a need. Over time, the agent associates the sight or smell of certain foods with the pleasure of drive reduction. This association strengthens the behavior further. 

Drive-induction (Curiosity): An innate need for novelty and challenge that can drive behavior. Can “trick” the release of drive satisfaction reward without reducing any arousal. Can reward an agent with new knowledge that helps future drive satisfaction. Can help prevent complacency which could cause agent death. 

Pain (Stimulation): A powerful reinforcer that can override every primary drive at once to protect the physical integrity/wellbeing of an agent.

Addiction: Repeating actions which historically resulted in drive satisfaction even if current results are diminished or absent. 


The key design principle is:

Primary drives create biological pressure. Secondary drives learn which people, places, objects, skills, roles, and beliefs are useful for reducing that pressure now or later.

1. Core Behavioral Model
Each agent should have:
- Needs — bodily or social conditions that can become deficient or threatened.
- Drives — the urgency/tension generated by those needs.
- Beliefs and pattern recognition — what the agent thinks will satisfy or protect a drive.
- Motives and goals — immediate or long-term actions selected to reduce present or anticipated drives.
- Emotions — fast evaluative states that alter action selection.
- Learning — reinforcement of actions that successfully reduce drives or avoid future deprivation.
- Traits and values — stable differences in thresholds, preferences, courage, patience, sociability, etc.

A useful abstraction: Action Value = Immediate Drive Reduction + Expected Future Drive Reduction + Social Reward − Risk − Effort − Opportunity Cost
An agent does not need to consciously understand this calculation. It can be implemented through weighted scores, learned associations, and heuristic decision rules.

2. Primary Drives
Primary drives are innate and tied directly to bodily integrity, survival, reproduction, or offspring survival. Not every primary drive must be represented as a separate meter; several can be grouped for simulation efficiency.

A. Metabolic and Physical Maintenance
Drive	Trigger	Satisfying Behaviors	Failure State
Hunger / energy need	Low calories, exertion, fasting	Forage, hunt, farm, trade, cook, eat, store food	Weakness, illness, death
Thirst / hydration need	Dehydration, heat, exertion	Drink, collect water, travel to water, trade	Impaired performance, death
Thermoregulation	Cold, heat, wetness, wind	Seek shelter, make fire, wear clothing, rest, migrate	Exposure, illness, death
Sleep / fatigue recovery	Wakefulness, labor, injury, stress	Sleep, rest, reduce activity, seek safe shelter	Poor performance, impaired judgment
Breathing / air quality	Suffocation, smoke, drowning, toxic air	Flee, surface, ventilate, seek clean air	Immediate incapacitation/death
Elimination / bodily comfort	Bladder/bowel pressure, hygiene need	Find privacy, clean self, use sanitation	Discomfort, disease risk, social penalty
Pain avoidance / bodily integrity	Injury, harmful stimulus, disease	Withdraw, defend, treat wound, rest, seek healer	Trauma, disability, death
Disease avoidance / immune protection	Contamination, parasites, symptoms, epidemic cues	Avoid spoiled food, isolate, wash, seek treatment	Illness, contagion, death
Physical activity / mobility	Stiffness, confinement, excess energy; less urgent	Move, exercise, explore, work	Reduced health, frustration, lower capability

Simulation note
Pain is not merely another need meter. It should be a high-priority interrupt signal capable of overriding hunger, social goals, curiosity, and even rest when immediate bodily damage is likely.

B. Safety and Security
These are partly biological and partly cognitive, but are fundamental enough to treat as primary or near-primary.

Drive	Trigger	Satisfying Behaviors	Failure State
Immediate safety / threat avoidance	Predator, attack, fire, collapse, violence	Flee, hide, fight, seek allies, fortify	Injury, death
Security / predictability	Resource insecurity, unstable shelter, hostile neighbors, debt, political danger	Store supplies, build shelter, join group, obey authority, relocate	Chronic stress, risk exposure
Control / agency	Entrapment, helplessness, inability to influence outcomes	Escape, acquire tools, gain skills, seek authority, resist coercion	Learned helplessness, passivity, rage
Resource reserve	Anticipation of future hunger, winter, drought, conflict	Hoard, save, preserve food, stockpile tools, accumulate wealth	Future deprivation vulnerability

Resource reserve is especially important for a primitive-to-medieval simulation. It creates behavior that otherwise looks irrational in the short term: storing grain instead of eating it, repairing a roof rather than hunting, acquiring land, or accumulating livestock.

C. Sexual, Reproductive, and Caregiving Drives
Drive	Trigger	Satisfying Behaviors	Failure State
Sexual desire	Hormonal state, attraction, learned cues, opportunity	Courtship, mating, pair-bonding behavior	Frustration, redirected effort, competition
Mate acquisition / retention	Desire for partnership, reproductive opportunity, attachment	Display competence, provide resources, affection, loyalty, jealousy, rivalry	Loneliness, status competition, conflict
Parental caregiving	Presence of dependent offspring, attachment, social expectations	Feed, protect, teach, comfort, provision	Offspring harm, grief, social penalties
Kin protection / kin investment	Kin need or threat	Share resources, defend relatives, arrange alliances	Loss of support network, grief, reputation loss
Family continuity / lineage	Desire for descendants, inheritance, legacy	Produce heirs, preserve family assets, train children, arrange marriages	Anxiety over status/property continuity

Not all individuals will prioritize reproduction equally. Personality, age, health, culture, opportunity, attachment, and learned values should alter these weights.

D. Attachment and Social Belonging
Humans are unusually dependent on groups. Social deprivation can impair survival even when food and shelter are available.

Drive	Trigger	Satisfying Behaviors	Failure State
Attachment	Separation from trusted persons; vulnerability	Seek family, friends, mentors, partners, familiar places	Distress, anxiety, grief
Belonging / inclusion	Isolation, exclusion, loss of group membership	Cooperate, conform, socialize, participate in rituals	Loneliness, vulnerability, shame
Acceptance / approval	Criticism, rejection, perceived disapproval	Apologize, comply, perform, seek reassurance	Shame, resentment, withdrawal
Reciprocity / fairness	Unequal exchange, betrayal, exploitation	Trade fairly, demand repayment, punish cheaters, share	Anger, distrust, feud
Status / rank	Inferior treatment, low influence, competition	Gain wealth, skill, prestige, office, followers, victory	Shame, envy, resentment
Recognition / esteem	Achievement ignored, competence questioned	Display skill, seek praise, acquire titles, public service	Frustration, status seeking
Group identity / loyalty	Threat to tribe, household, guild, faith, village, state	Defend group, follow norms, sacrifice for collective	Alienation, conflict, moral outrage

For a medieval society, a person may prioritize household, kin group, village, patron, guild, faith community, or lord over abstract individual preference. The exact hierarchy should vary between agents and cultures.

3. Primary Cognitive and Developmental Drives
These are not always “primary” in the narrow physiological sense, but they are sufficiently widespread and foundational to treat as innate motivational systems.

Drive	Trigger	Satisfying Behaviors	Long-Term Function
Curiosity / information seeking	Uncertainty, novelty, unexplained event	Explore, observe, ask, experiment, investigate	Improves prediction and survival
Competence / mastery	Inability, challenge, failed performance	Practice, train, build, learn, compete	Increases future drive satisfaction
Autonomy / self-direction	Coercion, dependence, blocked choice	Seek independence, resist control, gain resources/skills	Improves agency and resilience
Play	Safety plus surplus energy/time; developmental need	Explore, imitate, compete, invent	Learning, bonding, skill development
Meaning / coherence	Confusion, loss, suffering, contradiction	Create narratives, adopt beliefs, ritual, philosophy, religion	Supports persistence and coordination
Novelty / stimulation regulation	Boredom, monotony, under-stimulation	Explore, socialize, take risks, create	Prevents stagnation; discovers opportunities
Order / predictability	Chaos, uncertainty, lack of routine	Organize, plan, establish rules, ritualize	Reduces cognitive burden and risk

A useful distinction:
- Curiosity seeks information because information may improve future outcomes.
- Play explores low-cost actions and skills in relatively safe conditions.
- Mastery repeats and refines a valuable pattern until it becomes reliable.
- Meaning turns experiences into explanations, identities, and social commitments.

4. Secondary Drives
Secondary drives are learned motives. They become powerful because agents associate them with primary-drive satisfaction, threat avoidance, social rewards, identity, or future opportunity.

A secondary drive may outlast the original condition that created it. For example, an agent may pursue wealth long after they have enough food because wealth predicts safety, status, mating opportunity, influence, and family continuity.

5. Emotional States Are Not Usually Drives
Emotions should operate as decision modifiers. They assess whether a drive is threatened, attainable, blocked, or already satisfied.

Emotion	Typical Appraisal	Likely Behavioral Effect
Fear	Threat is high; success in confrontation seems unlikely	Flee, hide, submit, seek protection, freeze
Anger	A goal is blocked or a norm violated; confrontation seems possible	Fight, protest, punish, persist, dominate
Anxiety	Future threat is uncertain or difficult to predict	Prepare, avoid, seek information, ruminate
Sadness / grief	Loss of attachment, status, opportunity, or expected future	Withdraw, seek support, conserve effort, reassess
Shame	Social exposure or failure against group standards	Hide, apologize, repair reputation, submit
Guilt	Agent believes it harmed another or violated internalized norms	Repair harm, confess, compensate, change behavior
Envy	Another possesses a desired resource, role, or status	Compete, imitate, acquire, sometimes sabotage
Jealousy	Threat to valued relationship or mate bond	Guard, seek reassurance, confront rival
Pride	Success improves status, competence, or identity	Display, persist, lead, repeat behavior
Relief	A threat or deprivation has ended	Reinforce preceding action; reduce vigilance
Joy / contentment	Needs are satisfied and conditions are safe	Bond, explore, play, share, rest
Disgust	Contamination, disease risk, moral violation, or social aversion	Avoid, reject, cleanse, ostracize

Fear versus anger
The distinction is useful:
Fear: “This threat may stop me from satisfying my needs, and I probably cannot overcome it.”
Anger: “This threat may stop me from satisfying my needs, but I may be able to overcome or punish it.”
Freeze: threat is immediate, options unclear.
Appease/submit: resistance is unlikely to work but relationship preservation may.
Seek allies: threat is too large alone but manageable collectively.

6. Important Behavioral Systems That Are Not Simple Need Satisfaction
Overindulgence
Overindulgence occurs when an agent continues a rewarding action after the original need is satisfied.

Possible causes:
- The reward is unusually large, immediate, or reliable.
- The agent anticipates future scarcity.
- Another unsatisfied drive is being displaced onto an available reward.
- The action has acquired habit strength.
- The action provides social status, comfort, distraction, or stimulation.
- The agent has poor self-regulation because of fatigue, stress, intoxication, illness, or desperation.

Examples:
- Eating beyond hunger because food is scarce, pleasurable, or socially important.
- Hoarding wealth because wealth symbolizes safety and status.
- Drinking because it reduces anxiety or social discomfort.
- Working excessively because achievement temporarily reduces shame or insecurity.

Thrill-Seeking
Thrill-seeking should be represented as a tradeoff between:
- Risk and discomfort now
- Potential status, mastery, novelty, resource gain, or future security
- Arousal reward from successfully surviving danger

Agents more likely to thrill-seek may have:
- High novelty drive
- High status ambition
- High confidence or optimism
- Low perceived risk
- Low alternative opportunity
- Strong social rewards for bravery
- A history of successful risk-taking

Addiction and Compulsion
Addiction is best represented as a learned action whose expected reward remains high even when actual payoff declines.

Key variables:
- Historical reinforcement strength
- Cue sensitivity: places, people, smells, times, stressors
- Tolerance: less reward from the same action
- Withdrawal or discomfort when abstaining
- Habit automaticity
- Reduced sensitivity to delayed costs
- Competing-drive weakness under stress

This lets agents repeat behavior that no longer meets their needs because the old pattern remains strongly encoded.

7. Growth Drives
“Growth” is best treated as a category of investment behaviors: actions that sacrifice immediate satisfaction to improve future drive reduction.

Growth-related motives
Growth Action	Future Benefit
Learn a skill	Better food, income, status, autonomy
Train physically	Better safety, work capacity, mating/status potential
Build shelter	Better thermoregulation, security, sleep
Store food	Future hunger reduction
Maintain tools	Better future productivity
Form alliances	Future protection and trade
Raise children	Family continuity and future support
Teach apprentices	Labor capacity, legacy, group resilience
Explore	Maps, resources, opportunities, knowledge
Invest in reputation	Future cooperation and influence
Create institutions	Predictable norms, protection, coordination

For simulation purposes: Growth Motivation = Expected Future Benefit × Future Orientation × Confidence in Success
An agent with high hunger, immediate danger, or severe fatigue should discount long-term growth. A safe, well-fed agent should invest more in training, innovation, art, family, and institutions.

8. A Practical Drive Hierarchy for a Simulation
Rather than modeling dozens of independent meters, use a layered hierarchy.

Tier 1: Immediate Survival — Highest Priority
These can interrupt nearly any other activity.
- Air / escape from suffocation
- Acute injury and pain response
- Immediate lethal threat
- Severe thirst
- Severe cold, heat, or exposure
- Severe hunger
- Critical sleep deprivation
- Acute disease or poisoning

Tier 2: Near-Term Security
- Safe shelter
- Food and water reserves
- Protection from violence/predators
- Healing and hygiene
- Reliable access to tools and resources
- Social protection through family, group, patron, or alliance

Tier 3: Reproduction and Social Survival
- Attachment and belonging
- Mate seeking and mate retention
- Childcare and kin support
- Reputation and trust
- Status and influence
- Fairness, reciprocity, and norm enforcement

Tier 4: Capability and Future Opportunity
- Skill acquisition
- Curiosity and exploration
- Mastery and achievement
- Resource accumulation
- Autonomy and control
- Long-term planning and institution building

Tier 5: Meaning, Identity, and Self-Expression
- Faith, philosophy, and ritual
- Honor and duty
- Tradition and group identity
- Art and beauty
- Legacy
- Personal ideals and self-concept

Higher tiers do not disappear during deprivation. They become less behaviorally dominant unless they are strongly tied to identity or group survival. A starving parent may still sacrifice food for a child; a soldier may accept danger for honor, loyalty, or comrades; a desperate peasant may protect sacred property despite material loss.

9. Agent Differences Required for Human-Like Behavior
The same drives should produce different behavior in different agents. Give each agent parameters such as:

Trait	Effect
Risk tolerance	More likely to fight, explore, gamble, or flee late
Threat sensitivity	Notices danger sooner; higher fear response
Aggressiveness	More likely to use confrontation under frustration
Sociability	Gains more reward from proximity, conversation, teamwork
Attachment strength	More protective of family and friends
Ambition	Gives more weight to status, wealth, achievement
Empathy	Gives greater weight to others’ suffering and needs
Patience / future orientation	Will sacrifice now for later benefit
Curiosity	More likely to explore and experiment
Conformity	More likely to follow custom and group norms
Honor sensitivity	Reacts strongly to insult, humiliation, and reputation loss
Self-control	Better able to resist habits and immediate rewards
Confidence	More likely to attempt difficult or risky actions
Resilience	Recovers faster after loss, fear, failure, or pain
Religiosity or ideological commitment	Gives greater weight to ritual, moral norms, and transcendent meaning

These should vary by individual, developmental stage, health, social position, culture, and past experience.

10. Conditioning and Pattern Recognition
A behavior becomes more likely when an agent learns: Situation → Action → Drive Reduction

For example:
- Agent becomes hungry.
- Agent sees a river.
- Agent fishes.
- Agent catches food.
- Agent eats and hunger falls.
- The river, fishing tools, time of day, and prior success become cues.
- Next time hunger rises, fishing receives a higher action score.

A more advanced model should separately track:
- Expected payoff: “Fishing usually produces food.”
- Confidence: “How likely am I to succeed today?”
- Delay: “How long until I eat?”
- Cost: “How tiring or dangerous is fishing?”
- Alternatives: “Would trade, theft, farming, or hunting work better?”
- Social consequences: “Will fishing violate another person’s claim or obligation?”
- Memory strength: “Has this worked recently and often?”

This produces adaptation. An agent stops fishing when the river is depleted, weather is dangerous, or another method consistently works better.

12. Final Design Rule
Avoid treating agents as if they always choose the objectively best survival action. Human-like behavior comes from conflict among drives:
- Hunger competes with fear.
- Status competes with safety.
- Loyalty competes with self-interest.
- Curiosity competes with predictability.
- Immediate pleasure competes with future security.
- Anger competes with long-term cooperation.
- Care for family competes with personal survival.
- Meaning and honor can outweigh material gain.

That conflict is where believable behavior emerges.
